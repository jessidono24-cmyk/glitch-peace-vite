# Archaeology Research Sources

## Sources (minimum 10)

### [Renfrew & Bahn, 2012] — Archaeology: Theories, Methods, and Practice
**Full citation:** Renfrew, C., & Bahn, P. (2012). *Archaeology: Theories, Methods, and Practice* (6th ed.). Thames and Hudson.
**Key finding:** Archaeological knowledge is built from the stratigraphic record — the law of superposition holds that deeper layers are older; disturbance (bioturbation, looting) creates interpretive uncertainty.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — excavation layer system.
**Application:** The Archaeology mode presents a stratified grid; players excavate from the surface downward, and artifacts in lower layers are older and rarer — directly implementing the law of superposition as a game mechanic.

### [Harris, 1979] — Principles of Archaeological Stratigraphy
**Full citation:** Harris, E.C. (1979). *Principles of Archaeological Stratigraphy*. Academic Press.
**Key finding:** The Harris Matrix is a formal diagram of stratigraphic relationships; it unambiguously establishes chronological sequence even when physical layers are mixed.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — stratigraphic reasoning challenge.
**Application:** After excavation, players construct the Harris Matrix of their finds; correct matrix construction reveals the site's chronological narrative and unlocks the next excavation zone.

### [Trigger, 2006] — A History of Archaeological Thought
**Full citation:** Trigger, B.G. (2006). *A History of Archaeological Thought* (2nd ed.). Cambridge University Press.
**Key finding:** Archaeological interpretation is shaped by its cultural and political context; the same material can be interpreted through processual, post-processual, or cognitive-evolutionary lenses.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — multiple interpretation paths.
**Application:** Each major artifact find can be interpreted through three lenses (functional/processual, symbolic/post-processual, or evolutionary cognitive), each unlocking a different narrative branch — modeling Trigger's multi-paradigm framework.

### [Kenyon, 1957] — Digging Up Jericho
**Full citation:** Kenyon, K.M. (1957). *Digging Up Jericho*. Benn.
**Key finding:** Careful stratigraphic excavation of Jericho revealed 23 successive occupation layers spanning 10,000 years; each layer's material culture told a distinct story of a specific civilization.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — civilization layer narrative system.
**Application:** Each excavation site contains up to ten occupation layers; each layer's artifacts tell a story fragment. Completing all layers reveals the full dreamscape civilization history, mirroring Kenyon's layered narrative method.

### [Flannery, 1976] — The Early Mesoamerican Village
**Full citation:** Flannery, K.V. (Ed.) (1976). *The Early Mesoamerican Village*. Academic Press.
**Key finding:** Settlement pattern analysis — mapping the spatial distribution of sites — reveals social, economic, and political organization more reliably than individual site excavation alone.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — regional survey mapping.
**Application:** The regional survey sub-mode requires players to map artifact distributions across the dreamscape grid; correct spatial interpretation of the distribution reveals the settlement system's political structure.

### [Clark, 1954] — Excavations at Star Carr
**Full citation:** Clark, J.G.D. (1954). *Excavations at Star Carr*. Cambridge University Press.
**Key finding:** Mesolithic hunter-gatherer sites yield detailed evidence of seasonal occupation and diet from faunal remains; zooarchaeology can reconstruct annual subsistence cycles.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — faunal analysis challenge.
**Application:** Players identify animal bone assemblages to determine the season of occupation and dietary focus; correct zooarchaeological interpretation unlocks seasonal event content unique to that ancient culture.

### [Schliemann, 1880] — Ilios: The City and Country of the Trojans
**Full citation:** Schliemann, H. (1880). *Ilios: The City and Country of the Trojans*. Murray.
**Key finding:** Schliemann's determination that myth corresponded to real geography proved archaeology could be guided by literary sources — and also demonstrated the risks of theory-driven excavation damaging the record.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — myth-guided excavation mechanic.
**Application:** Players receive mythological clues (dreamscape legends) pointing to artifact locations; following them too literally risks missing neighboring layers, modeling both Schliemann's triumph and his well-documented stratigraphic errors.

### [Hodder, 1982] — Symbolic Structures and Spatial Patterning
**Full citation:** Hodder, I. (1982). *Symbols in Action: Ethnoarchaeological Studies of Material Culture*. Cambridge University Press.
**Key finding:** Material culture carries symbolic meaning that cannot be read from function alone; understanding artifact symbolism requires contextual interpretation grounded in living analogy.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — symbolic artifact interpretation.
**Application:** Each artifact has both a functional score and a symbolic meaning; players consult contextual clues (other artifacts in the same layer, spatial placement) to decode the symbolic meaning, following Hodder's contextual method.

### [Collingwood, 1946] — The Idea of History
**Full citation:** Collingwood, R.G. (1946). *The Idea of History*. Oxford University Press.
**Key finding:** Historical understanding requires re-enacting past thought — empathetically reconstructing how past actors understood their world, not imposing modern categories.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — culture-empathy scoring.
**Application:** Players who interpret artifacts using the cultural logic of the excavated civilization (rather than modern categories) receive a Culture Empathy bonus, rewarding Collingwood's historical reenactment approach.

### [Binford, 1962] — Archaeology as Anthropology
**Full citation:** Binford, L.R. (1962). Archaeology as anthropology. *American Antiquity*, 28(2), 217–225.
**Key finding:** Artifacts carry three dimensions of information: technomic (functional), sociotechnic (social/political), and ideotechnic (ideological/ritual) — each must be analyzed separately.
**Game mechanic:** Archaeology mode — three-axis artifact scoring.
**Application:** Every artifact is scored on Binford's three dimensions; players must categorize each dimension correctly. Missing any dimension gives partial credit; correctly assessing all three unlocks the full cultural-narrative entry.
