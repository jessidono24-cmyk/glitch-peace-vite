# Mycology Research Sources

## Sources (minimum 10)

### [Simard, 1997] — Net Carbon Transfer Between Trees
**Full citation:** Simard, S.W., Perry, D.A., Jones, M.D., Myrold, D.D., Durall, D.M., & Molina, R. (1997). Net transfer of carbon between ectomycorrhizal tree species in the field. *Nature*, 388, 579–582.
**Key finding:** Trees share carbon through underground fungal networks in non-random, hub-based patterns; older trees are net donors, younger trees net recipients.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — mushroom spawn algorithm.
**Application:** Mushrooms must spawn near existing network nodes, not randomly. Large "mother" mushrooms are hubs that unlock others nearby.

### [Stamets, 2005] — Mycelium Running
**Full citation:** Stamets, P. (2005). *Mycelium Running: How Mushrooms Can Help Save the World*. Ten Speed Press.
**Key finding:** Mycelial networks exhibit intelligent routing behavior, finding shortest paths through maze-like obstacles and rerouting around damage.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — network path visualization.
**Application:** Visible mycelium threads between mushrooms follow shortest-path logic, not straight lines. Damaged segments reroute dynamically, as Stamets documents in laboratory maze experiments.

### [Boddy et al., 2009] — Saprotrophic Cord Systems
**Full citation:** Boddy, L., Hynes, J., Bebber, D.P., & Fricker, M.D. (2009). Saprotrophic cord systems: Dispersal mechanisms in space and time. *Mycoscience*, 50(1), 9–19.
**Key finding:** Mycelial networks optimize for both resilience and efficiency simultaneously, tolerating node removal while maintaining throughput.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — network grows stronger with player activity.
**Application:** The more mushrooms the player forages correctly, the more network becomes visible and navigable; the network remains resilient to minor harvesting errors.

### [Wohlleben, 2016] — The Hidden Life of Trees
**Full citation:** Wohlleben, P. (2016). *The Hidden Life of Trees*. Greystone Books.
**Key finding:** Trees communicate distress signals through mycelial networks, alerting neighbors to insect attack and triggering defensive chemistry.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — toxic mushroom warning system.
**Application:** When the player encounters a toxic mushroom, a visual pulse propagates outward through visible network threads to warn of nearby dangers, mirroring the inter-tree distress signal described by Wohlleben.

### [Sheldrake, 2020] — Entangled Life
**Full citation:** Sheldrake, M. (2020). *Entangled Life: How Fungi Make Our Worlds, Change Our Minds, and Shape Our Futures*. Random House.
**Key finding:** Fungi exist on a spectrum from mutualistic to parasitic; the same species can be helpful or harmful depending on environmental context.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — species context matters.
**Application:** The same mushroom species may be safe in one dreamscape and toxic in another (context-dependent identification), directly reflecting Sheldrake's finding about contextual mutualism.

### [Kiers et al., 2011] — Reciprocal Rewards Stabilize Cooperation
**Full citation:** Kiers, E.T., Duhamel, M., Beesetty, Y., Mensah, J.A., Franken, O., Verbruggen, E., … Bücking, H. (2011). Reciprocal rewards stabilize cooperation in the mycorrhizal symbiosis. *Science*, 333(6044), 880–882.
**Key finding:** Mycorrhizal networks preferentially reward fungi that deliver more nutrients — a biological market-like fairness system.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — player receives more network visibility the more carefully they forage (reciprocity mechanic).
**Application:** Careful, correct identification = expanded network vision. Greedy or wrong collection = network dims. This mirrors the reciprocal-reward market documented by Kiers et al.

### [Beiler et al., 2010] — Architecture of the Wood-Wide Web
**Full citation:** Beiler, K.J., Durall, D.M., Simard, S.W., Maxwell, S.A., & Kretzer, A.M. (2010). Architecture of the wood-wide web: *Rhizopogon* spp. genets link multiple Douglas-fir cohorts. *New Phytologist*, 185(2), 543–553.
**Key finding:** Fungal networks show scale-free topology with a few highly connected hub trees and many peripheral nodes with few connections.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — network topology design.
**Application:** A few "mother" mushroom nodes have many connections; most mushrooms have 2–3 connections. Hub mushrooms provide larger score bonuses, reflecting their outsized ecological role.

### [Fricker et al., 2017] — The Mathematical Basis of Mycelial Growth
**Full citation:** Fricker, M., Heaton, L., Jones, N., & Boddy, L. (2017). The mycelium as a network. *Microbiology Spectrum*, 5(3), FUNK-0033-2016.
**Key finding:** Mycelial growth follows mathematical optimization principles similar to slime molds solving shortest-path problems; branching patterns are computationally predictable.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — difficulty scaling.
**Application:** In higher-difficulty dreamscapes, the mushroom network becomes more complex and requires understanding of branching patterns, guided by the mathematical rules Fricker documents.

### [Dighton, 2016] — Fungi in Ecosystem Services
**Full citation:** Dighton, J. (2016). *Fungi in Ecosystem Services*. CRC Press.
**Key finding:** Fungi are keystone species — removing them collapses ecosystem nutrient cycling, with cascading failure across trophic levels.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — consequence system.
**Application:** Overharvesting (foraging too fast or incorrectly) destabilizes the network — mushrooms stop spawning, threads disappear. Rest and patience restore the system, modeling Dighton's keystone-species dynamics.

### [Gorzelak et al., 2015] — Inter-plant Communication Through Mycorrhizal Networks
**Full citation:** Gorzelak, M.A., Asay, A.K., Pickles, B.J., & Simard, S.W. (2015). Inter-plant communication through mycorrhizal networks mediates complex adaptive behaviour in plant communities. *AoB Plants*, 7, plv050.
**Key finding:** Mycorrhizal networks transmit defense signals between unconnected plants, conferring systemic resistance before an attacker even reaches the neighbor.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — stress signal propagation.
**Application:** When a toxic mushroom is encountered, the network broadcasts a warning pulse to all connected nodes, allowing the player to prepare — a direct game translation of Gorzelak's pre-emptive defense signaling.

### [Sheldrake & Fricker, 2020] — Wood Wide Web
**Full citation:** Sheldrake, M., & Fricker, M. (2020). Wood wide web: Underground forest communication. *Nature Plants*, 6, 106–107.
**Key finding:** Network intelligence is distributed — there is no central control point; collective behavior emerges from local interactions.
**Game mechanic:** Mycology mode — awe/wonder effect trigger on hub discovery.
**Application:** When the player discovers a major hub mushroom, the "profound_awe" effect is triggered — text reads: "This organism has been growing for centuries. Its network spans the entire dreamscape. You are a guest." This honors the distributed intelligence Sheldrake and Fricker describe.
