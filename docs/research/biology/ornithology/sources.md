# Ornithology Research Sources

## Sources (minimum 10)

### [Sibley, 2000] — The Sibley Guide to Birds
**Full citation:** Sibley, D.A. (2000). *The Sibley Guide to Birds*. Knopf.
**Key finding:** Expert birders use a hierarchical identification system: size/shape first, then behavior, then habitat, then plumage — rarely relying on any single cue.
**Game mechanic:** Ornithology mode — multi-cue identification system.
**Application:** The game presents clues in the Sibley hierarchy: silhouette (size/shape) appears first, then movement animation, then habitat tile, then color — training authentic field-identification cognition.

### [Hutchinson, 1957] — Concluding Remarks on the Ecological Niche
**Full citation:** Hutchinson, G.E. (1957). Concluding remarks. *Cold Spring Harbor Symposia on Quantitative Biology*, 22, 415–427.
**Key finding:** Every species occupies an n-dimensional hypervolume of environmental conditions (the ecological niche); species can only persist within their niche tolerances.
**Game mechanic:** Ornithology mode — habitat-based species filtering.
**Application:** Each dreamscape biome (forest, wetland, desert, arctic) filters which species can appear, accurately reflecting niche theory — a marsh species never appears in a desert level.

### [Alerstam, 1990] — Bird Migration
**Full citation:** Alerstam, T. (1990). *Bird Migration*. Cambridge University Press.
**Key finding:** Migratory timing is under endogenous circannual control but is calibrated by photoperiod; many species travel within predictable seasonal windows.
**Game mechanic:** Migration timing bonuses — seasonal event mechanics.
**Application:** Species during their real-world migration windows (spring/fall) appear more frequently in game and yield bonus points, rewarding players who learn actual phenology.

### [Marler, 1970] — A Comparative Approach to Vocal Learning
**Full citation:** Marler, P. (1970). A comparative approach to vocal learning: Song development in white-crowned sparrows. *Journal of Comparative and Physiological Psychology Monographs*, 71(2, Pt. 2), 1–25.
**Key finding:** Songbirds have a sensitive period for song learning; they memorize a template during the sensitive period and match output to template through practice.
**Game mechanic:** Vocalization clues — audio identification challenges.
**Application:** The audio challenge presents partial birdsong, requiring players to complete the pattern — mirroring the template-matching process Marler describes, and rewarding song-memory development.

### [Keltner & Haidt, 2003] — Approaching Awe, a Moral, Spiritual, and Aesthetic Emotion
**Full citation:** Keltner, D., & Haidt, J. (2003). Approaching awe, a moral, spiritual, and aesthetic emotion. *Cognition and Emotion*, 17(2), 297–314.
**Key finding:** Awe involves perceived vastness and the need for accommodation of existing mental structures; encounters with rare or extraordinary animals reliably trigger awe.
**Game mechanic:** Rare bird encounter awe effect.
**Application:** When the player correctly identifies a rare or apex species, the game triggers the "profound_awe" state: slow motion, reverb audio, and text noting the rarity — operationalizing Keltner & Haidt's awe model.

### [Cornell Lab of Ornithology — eBird, 2022]
**Full citation:** Sullivan, B.L., et al. (2009). eBird: A citizen-based bird observation network in the biological sciences. *Biological Conservation*, 142(10), 2282–2292.
**Key finding:** eBird frequency data reveals that most species have characteristic detectability curves across the year, with predictable peaks aligned with breeding and migration.
**Game mechanic:** Rarity scoring system.
**Application:** Species rarity scores are derived from eBird frequency data, ensuring that common species score low and genuinely rare species score high, grounding the game in real population data.

### [Prum, 2017] — The Evolution of Beauty
**Full citation:** Prum, R.O. (2017). *The Evolution of Beauty: How Darwin's Forgotten Theory of Mate Choice Reshapes the Animal World and Us*. Doubleday.
**Key finding:** Many bird traits evolved through aesthetic mate selection independent of survival advantage; birds are active aesthetic agents.
**Game mechanic:** Ornithology mode — plumage scoring bonus for elaborate species.
**Application:** Species with elaborate plumage (birds-of-paradise, hummingbirds) score aesthetic bonus points in addition to rarity points, honoring Prum's theory that beauty is intrinsically significant.

### [Fagen, 1981] — Animal Play Behavior
**Full citation:** Fagen, R. (1981). *Animal Play Behavior*. Oxford University Press.
**Key finding:** Corvids, parrots, and raptors engage in play behavior — inverted flight, object manipulation — that is positively correlated with brain size and ecological flexibility.
**Game mechanic:** Ornithology mode — behavior-clue animations.
**Application:** Corvid and parrot encounters include play-behavior animations (rolling, object dropping) as identification clues, reflecting Fagen's documentation of avian play as a species-characteristic behavior.

### [Newton, 1998] — Population Limitation in Birds
**Full citation:** Newton, I. (1998). *Population Limitation in Birds*. Academic Press.
**Key finding:** Bird populations are regulated by resource bottlenecks — particularly food availability in winter and nest sites in spring — that create predictable density patterns.
**Game mechanic:** Habitat quality scoring — biome health affects species diversity.
**Application:** Dreamscapes with degraded habitat tiles (DESPAIR clusters) support fewer species; restoring the grid with PEACE tiles increases species availability, modeling Newton's resource-limitation principle.

### [Gill, 2007] — Ornithology
**Full citation:** Gill, F.B. (2007). *Ornithology* (3rd ed.). Freeman.
**Key finding:** Birds use a minimum of four sensory modalities in navigation: magnetic fields, star patterns, sun compass, and olfaction — showing multi-modal sensory integration.
**Game mechanic:** Ornithology mode — multi-modal clue system.
**Application:** Advanced identification challenges provide multi-modal cues (visual + audio + habitat + behavior), training the multi-sensory integration that Gill documents as central to avian cognition.
