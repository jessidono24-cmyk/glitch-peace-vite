# Engineering Research Sources

## Sources (minimum 10)

### [Senge, 1990] — The Fifth Discipline
**Full citation:** Senge, P.M. (1990). *The Fifth Discipline: The Art and Practice of the Learning Organization*. Doubleday.
**Key finding:** Systems thinking — the fifth discipline — reveals feedback loops, delays, and unintended consequences invisible to linear cause-effect thinking; archetypes like "fixes that fail" recur across systems.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — systems feedback loop design.
**Application:** Engineering challenges present systems with hidden feedback loops; players must identify the system archetype (balancing loop, reinforcing loop, delay) and intervene at high-leverage points to stabilize the system.

### [Meadows, 2008] — Thinking in Systems
**Full citation:** Meadows, D.H. (2008). *Thinking in Systems: A Primer*. Chelsea Green.
**Key finding:** System behavior is determined by structure (stocks, flows, feedback loops), not by external events; leverage points for change are often counterintuitive.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — leverage-point identification.
**Application:** Each engineering puzzle has a hidden leverage point; discovering it produces disproportionate improvement. The game scores players on whether they found the leverage point vs. brute-forcing the system — per Meadows.

### [Norman, 1988] — The Design of Everyday Things
**Full citation:** Norman, D.A. (1988). *The Design of Everyday Things*. Basic Books.
**Key finding:** Good design communicates affordances (what actions are possible) and feedback (what effect occurred); bad design produces errors through false affordances and missing feedback.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — affordance design challenges.
**Application:** Players must engineer game interfaces that correctly signal affordances; objects with misleading affordances cause "user error" events, scoring the player down — directly applying Norman's design principles.

### [Wiener, 1948] — Cybernetics
**Full citation:** Wiener, N. (1948). *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*. MIT Press.
**Key finding:** Goal-directed behavior in machines and organisms requires negative feedback; control is information-driven, not energy-driven.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — negative feedback control loops.
**Application:** Engineering challenges require players to add negative feedback regulators to unstable systems; without feedback, systems oscillate or diverge — modeling Wiener's foundational cybernetic principle.

### [Petroski, 1992] — To Engineer Is Human
**Full citation:** Petroski, H. (1992). *To Engineer Is Human: The Role of Failure in Successful Design*. Vintage.
**Key finding:** Engineering advances through failure analysis; most great engineering innovations were responses to understanding prior catastrophic failures.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — failure analysis scoring bonus.
**Application:** When a player's structure fails, a detailed failure analysis mini-game unlocks; correctly diagnosing the failure mode earns bonus points, rewarding Petroski's failure-as-learning principle.

### [Dijkstra, 1959] — A Note on Two Problems in Connexion with Graphs
**Full citation:** Dijkstra, E.W. (1959). A note on two problems in connexion with graphs. *Numerische Mathematik*, 1(1), 269–271.
**Key finding:** Shortest-path algorithms (Dijkstra's algorithm) optimally route information through networks in O(E + V log V) time; efficiency is a computable, optimizable property.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — network routing optimization.
**Application:** Network-routing engineering challenges require players to minimize path length; the game uses Dijkstra's algorithm to compute the optimal solution and scores players on how close their design comes to the theoretical minimum.

### [Shannon, 1948] — A Mathematical Theory of Communication
**Full citation:** Shannon, C.E. (1948). A mathematical theory of communication. *Bell System Technical Journal*, 27(3), 379–423.
**Key finding:** The theoretical maximum information throughput of a noisy channel (channel capacity) is determined by bandwidth and signal-to-noise ratio — the Shannon limit.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — communication channel design.
**Application:** Communication channel challenges ask players to maximize throughput of a noisy dreamscape link; the Shannon limit serves as the scoring ceiling, teaching players that channel capacity is a fundamental physical constraint.

### [Rasmussen, 1983] — Skills, Rules, and Knowledge
**Full citation:** Rasmussen, J. (1983). Skills, rules, and knowledge; signals, signs, and symbols, and other distinctions in human performance models. *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics*, SMC-13(3), 257–266.
**Key finding:** Human performance in complex systems operates at three levels — skill-based (automatic), rule-based (procedural), and knowledge-based (analytical) — with errors characteristic of each level.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — multi-level operator simulation.
**Application:** Engineering systems require action at all three Rasmussen levels simultaneously: automatic monitoring (skill), procedure following (rule), and novel problem solving (knowledge) — creating rich, layered cognitive demands.

### [Ackoff, 1999] — Ackoff's Best
**Full citation:** Ackoff, R.L. (1999). *Ackoff's Best: His Classic Writings on Management*. Wiley.
**Key finding:** The performance of a system depends more on how its parts interact than on how they perform independently; optimization of parts often sub-optimizes the whole.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — subsystem interaction scoring.
**Application:** Players who optimize each subsystem independently score less than players who optimize the whole-system interaction — Ackoff's sub-optimization principle rendered as a scoring mechanic.

### [MacKay, 2003] — Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms
**Full citation:** MacKay, D.J.C. (2003). *Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms*. Cambridge University Press. (Free: https://www.inference.org.uk/itila/)
**Key finding:** Error-correcting codes allow reliable communication over unreliable channels by adding structured redundancy; the overhead cost is bounded by the channel capacity.
**Game mechanic:** Engineering mode — error correction challenge.
**Application:** Players must design a redundancy scheme for a noisy dreamscape communication link; the minimum redundancy to achieve reliable transmission is computed from Shannon's bound and serves as the scoring target.
