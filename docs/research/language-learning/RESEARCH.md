## SECTION A — How Polyglots Actually Learn

### [Erard, 2012] — Babel No More
**Full citation:** Erard, M. (2012). *Babel No More: The Search for the World's Most Extraordinary Language Learners*. Free Press.
**Key finding:** Most hyperpolyglots share early musical training, strong phonological memory, and treat language as daily maintenance rather than a course to complete.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** Daily session streak system; Rhythm mode integration.
**Application:** The streak system frames language learning as a daily maintenance practice, not a finite course. Musical rhythm integration is grounded in shared hyperpolyglot trait of early musical training and phonological sensitivity.

---

### [Bialystok, Craik & Freedman, 2007] — Bilingualism as Protection Against Dementia
**Full citation:** Bialystok, E., Craik, F.I.M., & Freedman, M. (2007). Bilingualism as a protection against the onset of symptoms of dementia. *Neuropsychologia, 45*(2), 459–464.
**Key finding:** Bilingualism was associated with delayed onset of dementia symptoms by approximately four years in a clinical sample, suggesting a cognitive reserve effect.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Matrix A/B toggle as attentional switching mechanic.
**Application:** The toggle trains inhibitory control and attentional switching — mechanisms consistent with bilingual cognitive exercise — without overclaiming guaranteed brain benefits, given replication failures (see Section I).

---

### [Paap et al., 2015] — The Bilingual Advantage Debate: Null Results
**Full citation:** Paap, K.R., Johnson, H.A., & Sawi, O. (2015). Bilingual advantages in executive functioning either do not exist or are restricted to specific circumstances. *Cortex, 73*, 287–310.
**Key finding:** A large-scale review found that the majority of published studies testing executive function advantages in bilinguals yield null results, calling the bilingual advantage claim into question.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Matrix A/B toggle framing.
**Application:** The mechanic is framed as attentional exercise, not as a guaranteed cognitive enhancer. Both the claim and its replication failures are represented honestly in game copy and onboarding text.

---

### [Lomb, 2008] — How I Learn Languages
**Full citation:** Lomb, K. (2008). *How I Learn Languages*. TESL-EJ Publications. (Original: *Így tanulok nyelveket*, 1970.)
**Key finding:** Reading for genuine pleasure early in learning, always encountering vocabulary in full context, and using the target language as a tool from day one rather than as an object of study produces durable acquisition.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** In-game vocabulary always appears in a full sentence, never isolated.
**Application:** Every quiz card and ambient word display includes a complete example sentence. Isolated word lists are never shown to the player.

---

### [Machová, 2018] — Language Learning Secrets (TED Talk)
**Full citation:** Machová, L. (2018). *The secrets of learning a new language* [TED Talk]. TED.com; ongoing coaching material 2018–present.
**Key finding:** No single best method exists for language learning — the best method is whichever one the learner enjoys enough to sustain for 500 or more hours.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** Player selects learning style (immersion-first / structured / output-first).
**Application:** The learning style selector at mode entry acknowledges method diversity as a feature. Players who prefer output-first see more production quizzes earlier; immersion-first players spend more time in ambient layer before quizzes begin.

---

### [Lampariello, 2010–present] — Bidirectional Translation
**Full citation:** Lampariello, L. (2010–present). Practitioner writing and video content on bidirectional translation methodology. LucaLampariello.com.
**Key finding:** Translating native-language text into the target language, then back without looking at the original, forces output-driven retrieval that maps directly to the testing effect.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Bidirectional recall challenge in Layer 3 production mode.
**Application:** Layer 3 includes a bidirectional recall challenge where players produce the target-language form from the native form, then verify native reconstruction — mirroring Lampariello's practitioner method and Roediger & Karpicke's testing-effect research.

---

### [Simcott, 2010–present] — Phonological Accuracy and Language Family Grouping
**Full citation:** Simcott, R. (2010–present). Practitioner documentation: interviews, LinguaFest keynotes, YouTube channel (multilingualrichard). Multiple languages spoken.
**Key finding:** Prioritizing phonological accuracy early and grouping closely related language families together (Romance, Slavic, Germanic) accelerates acquisition by leveraging cross-language transfer effects.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Language family grouping in the language selector.
**Application:** The language selector groups available languages by family. Players who already know one Romance language are shown transfer-percentage estimates to related targets, making family proximity a visible, motivating feature.

---

### [Kaufmann, 2007–present] — LingQ Methodology: Massive Comprehensible Input
**Full citation:** Kaufmann, S. (2007–present). LingQ platform methodology; YouTube channel (Steve Kaufmann — lingosteve). Content-driven extensive reading/listening system.
**Key finding:** Massive content-driven exposure through extensive reading and listening — prioritizing meaning before formal grammar — maps directly to Nation's comprehensible input research and produces vocabulary at scale.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** Dreamscape narration layer in target language as passive immersion.
**Application:** The dreamscape narration and ambient text layer constitutes the passive immersion channel. Players absorb target-language narration during gameplay without explicit study, replicating Kaufmann's content-first methodology.

---

### [Arguelles, 2000–present] — Shadowing Methodology
**Full citation:** Arguelles, A. (2000–present). Shadowing methodology documentation; YouTube channel (alexanderarg); academic and practitioner writing on outread shadowing.
**Key finding:** Outread shadowing — listening to audio while reading text simultaneously, spoken aloud — builds the phonological circuit faster than passive listening alone, and is distinct from simple repetition.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Audio + text simultaneous display on interlude screens.
**Application:** Interlude screens display target-language text synchronized with audio narration. Players are prompted to speak along, implementing the simultaneous audio-text-output triad Arguelles identifies as the core of effective shadowing.

---

### [Bialystok, 2001] — Metalinguistic Awareness in Multilinguals
**Full citation:** Bialystok, E. (2001). *Bilingualism in Development: Language, Literacy, and Cognition*. Cambridge University Press.
**Key finding:** Experienced multilinguals develop heightened metalinguistic awareness — the capacity to treat language as an object of analysis — which accelerates acquisition of each additional language.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** D/S/R memory-state display after each review.
**Application:** Displaying Difficulty, Stability, and Retrievability values after each review builds explicit metacognitive awareness of one's own memory state, reinforcing the kind of analytical attention to language that experienced multilinguals naturally develop.

---

### [Cenoz, 2003] — L3 Transfer and Related-Language Acceleration
**Full citation:** Cenoz, J. (2003). The additive effect of bilingualism on third language acquisition: A review. *International Journal of Bilingualism, 7*(1), 71–87.
**Key finding:** L3 acquisition is accelerated relative to L2 acquisition, particularly when the L3 is typologically related to either the L1 or L2, due to positive cross-language transfer effects.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Language family grouping in the language selector; transfer-percentage display.
**Application:** Players who have already acquired one language in the selector see a cross-language transfer estimate for typologically close targets, grounding the family-grouping feature in L3 transfer research rather than practitioner intuition alone.

---

### [de Bruin et al., 2015] — Replication Failures in Bilingual Advantage Research
**Full citation:** de Bruin, A., Treccani, B., & Della Sala, S. (2015). Cognitive advantage in bilingualism: An example of publication bias? *Psychological Science, 26*(1), 99–107.
**Key finding:** A review of conference abstracts and published papers found substantial publication bias in bilingual advantage research — null results were disproportionately unpublished, inflating the apparent effect size in the literature.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Matrix A/B toggle framing; onboarding copy.
**Application:** The publication-bias finding strengthens the rationale for avoiding cognitive-enhancement claims in player-facing copy. The bilingual advantage may partly be an artifact of selective publication rather than a robust population effect.

---

### Note on SLA Research Population Bias
Most second-language acquisition (SLA) research uses beginner monolingual learners as participants, because institutional access (classrooms) makes this the convenient sample. This creates a systematic bias: findings optimized for L1→L2 beginners may not generalize to experienced multilinguals acquiring L3+, adult learners with high metalinguistic awareness, or learners motivated by intrinsic rather than institutional goals. The Language Learning mode is explicitly designed for the experienced, self-directed learner population described by polyglot practitioners — not for the institutional beginner described by most SLA literature. Where research findings conflict between these populations, the practitioner evidence base is given priority.

## SECTION B — Spaced Repetition and Memory Architecture

> **Why FSRS, not SM-2:** SM-2 (Wozniak & Gorzelanczyk, 1994) achieves approximately 47% benchmark retention. FSRS (Ye, 2023, ACM KDD) achieves approximately 90% retention with 20–30% fewer total reviews by modeling per-card Difficulty, Stability, and Retrievability using a power forgetting curve fitted to individual review history. SM-2 is obsolete for any serious implementation.

---

### [Ebbinghaus, 1885/1913] — The Forgetting Curve
**Full citation:** Ebbinghaus, H. (1885/1913). *Memory: A contribution to experimental psychology*. Teachers College, Columbia University. (Trans. Ruger & Bussenius.)
**Key finding:** Without review, memory for newly learned material decays exponentially — approximately 50% lost within one hour and 70% within 24 hours under controlled conditions.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** FSRS scheduler timing; limited new words per session.
**Application:** The FSRS scheduler times each review to occur just before the learner's personal forgetting threshold. The daily session cap on new words prevents overloading working memory beyond the Ebbinghaus decay horizon before sleep consolidation can occur.

---

### [Wozniak & Gorzelanczyk, 1994] — SM-2 Optimization of Repetition Spacing
**Full citation:** Wozniak, P.A., & Gorzelanczyk, E.J. (1994). Optimization of repetition spacing in the practice of learning. *Acta Neurobiologiae Experimentalis, 54*(1), 59–62.
**Key finding:** Spaced repetition with expanding intervals dramatically improves long-term retention over massed practice, with a specific algorithm (SM-2) producing measurable improvement in vocabulary learning.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Basis for understanding why spaced repetition is used; historical context for FSRS improvement.
**Application:** SM-2 established the proof of concept for algorithmic spaced repetition. FSRS supersedes it by adding per-card memory modeling; the scheduler implementation uses FSRS-5, not SM-2, for the approximately 43-percentage-point retention improvement.

---

### [Cepeda et al., 2006] — Distributed Practice in Verbal Recall
**Full citation:** Cepeda, N.J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J.T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin, 132*(3), 354–380.
**Key finding:** Spaced practice robustly outperforms massed practice for long-term retention across hundreds of experiments — the spacing effect is one of the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** FSRS scheduler; prohibition on binge-review of mastered cards.
**Application:** The game does not allow players to review already-stable cards on demand. Scheduling control remains with the FSRS algorithm, enforcing genuine spacing rather than comfort-review.

---

### [Cepeda et al., 2008] — Optimal Inter-Study Interval
**Full citation:** Cepeda, N.J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J.T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. *Psychological Science, 19*(11), 1095–1102.
**Key finding:** The optimal spacing interval increases as the desired retention interval increases — there is a specific ratio between study-gap and test-gap that maximizes efficiency, and FSRS operationalizes a close approximation of this ratio.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** FSRS Stability (S) parameter growth rate.
**Application:** The S parameter in FSRS models the growing interval required to maintain retrievability at the target threshold, directly implementing the optimal inter-study interval logic established by Cepeda et al.

---

### [Roediger & Karpicke, 2006] — Test-Enhanced Learning
**Full citation:** Roediger, H.L., & Karpicke, J.D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science, 17*(3), 249–255.
**Key finding:** Retrieval practice (testing) produces substantially greater long-term retention than re-reading or re-studying the same material, an effect that holds across content types.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** Quiz layers (Layer 2 recognition, Layer 3 production); retrieval over re-study design.
**Application:** The game never shows players a word without asking for retrieval. Ambient immersion (Layer 1) is exposure; quizzes (Layers 2–3) are the retention mechanism. Re-reading a definition is never presented as a study option.

---

### [Kornell & Bjork, 2008] — Interleaving and Category Learning
**Full citation:** Kornell, N., & Bjork, R.A. (2008). Learning concepts and categories: Is spacing the "enemy of induction"? *Psychological Science, 19*(6), 585–592.
**Key finding:** Spaced, interleaved practice produces better category-learning and generalization than blocked practice, even though learners typically prefer and predict blocked practice will be more effective.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Interleaved vocabulary presentation across semantic categories.
**Application:** Vocabulary from different semantic clusters (nature, urban, body, action) is interleaved within quiz sessions rather than presented in thematic blocks, improving discrimination and generalization despite feeling less intuitive to players.

---

### [Dunlosky et al., 2013] — Effective Learning Techniques
**Full citation:** Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K.A., Marsh, E.J., Nathan, M.J., & Willingham, D.T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14*(1), 4–58.
**Key finding:** Practice testing and distributed practice are the two highest-utility learning techniques identified across a systematic review of the cognitive psychology literature; rereading and highlighting rank among the least effective.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Overall design principle: retrieval-based learning with spaced repetition.
**Application:** The game's layer architecture — immersion then retrieval, not re-reading — directly implements the two highest-utility techniques identified by Dunlosky et al. and deliberately avoids passive review mechanics.

---

### [Ye, 2023] — FSRS: A Stochastic Shortest Path Algorithm
**Full citation:** Ye, J. (2023). A stochastic shortest path algorithm for optimizing spaced repetition scheduling. *Proceedings of the 29th ACM SIGKDD Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining (ACM KDD 2023)*.
**Key finding:** The FSRS scheduler, modeled as a stochastic shortest path problem over a per-card Difficulty–Stability–Retrievability space, achieves approximately 90% retention with 20–30% fewer reviews than SM-2's approximately 47% benchmark.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** Core FSRS-5 scheduler (D/S/R model); post-review memory state display.
**Application:** FSRS-5 is the sole scheduler used in the Language Learning mode. D, S, and R values are displayed after each review so players understand their own memory state, implementing the transparency principle informed by hyperpolyglot metacognition research.

## SECTION C — Comprehensible Input and Immersion

### [Krashen, 1982] — Principles and Practice in SLA
**Full citation:** Krashen, S.D. (1982). *Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition*. Pergamon Press.
**Key finding:** Language acquisition occurs through exposure to comprehensible input slightly above the learner's current level (i+1); explicit grammar study feeds the Monitor but does not drive acquisition directly.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** Three-layer model (immersion → recognition → production); i+1 difficulty scaling; ambient immersion layer.
**Application:** The immersion layer (Layer 1) provides ambient comprehensible input without quiz pressure. The difficulty scaling targets i+1 by mixing known and unknown vocabulary in ambient display, implementing Krashen's acquisition-not-learning distinction.

---

### [Krashen, 1985] — The Input Hypothesis
**Full citation:** Krashen, S.D. (1985). *The Input Hypothesis: Issues and Implications*. Longman.
**Key finding:** The input hypothesis formalizes the claim that acquisition requires comprehensible input at i+1, and that the affective filter — anxiety, low motivation — blocks acquisition even when input is available.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Low-pressure immersion layer design; no failure state in Layer 1.
**Application:** Layer 1 has no quiz, no failure state, and no score. This minimizes the affective filter, implementing Krashen's prediction that acquisition requires low-anxiety conditions.

---

### [VanPatten, 1996] — Input Processing and Grammar Instruction
**Full citation:** VanPatten, B. (1996). *Input Processing and Grammar Instruction: Theory and Research*. Ablex Publishing.
**Key finding:** Learners process meaning before form; instruction that forces attention to form during meaning-focused input (structured input activities) improves acquisition compared to traditional grammar explanation.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** In-context vocabulary display; form highlighted within sentence.
**Application:** Vocabulary is never presented as a naked form–meaning pair. The target word is highlighted within a full example sentence, directing form-attention while preserving meaning context, implementing VanPatten's structured input principle.

---

### [Nation, 2001] — Learning Vocabulary in Another Language
**Full citation:** Nation, I.S.P. (2001). *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language*. Cambridge University Press.
**Key finding:** Durable vocabulary acquisition requires coverage across four strands — meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning, and fluency development — each contributing distinct aspects of word knowledge.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** In-context vocabulary (sentences not lists); three-layer architecture covering all four strands.
**Application:** Layer 1 covers meaning-focused input; Layer 2 covers language-focused learning; Layer 3 covers meaning-focused output. Fluency development occurs through accumulated session exposure over time.

---

### [Laufer & Nation, 1999] — Vocabulary-Size Test of Controlled Productive Ability
**Full citation:** Laufer, B., & Nation, P. (1999). A vocabulary-size test of controlled productive ability. *Language Testing, 16*(1), 33–51.
**Key finding:** Receptive vocabulary knowledge does not automatically translate to productive ability; controlled productive knowledge is a distinct and harder-to-achieve level requiring explicit production practice.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Separate Layer 2 (recognition) and Layer 3 (production) quiz types.
**Application:** Recognition quizzes and production quizzes are tracked separately in the FSRS scheduler, implementing Laufer and Nation's finding that receptive and productive vocabulary are distinct knowledge states requiring independent practice.

---

### [Hu & Nation, 2000] — Vocabulary Density and Reading Comprehension
**Full citation:** Hu, M., & Nation, I.S.P. (2000). Vocabulary density and reading comprehension. *Reading in a Foreign Language, 23*(1).
**Key finding:** Readers need to know approximately 98% of the words in a text to achieve adequate comprehension without assistance — below this threshold, comprehension degrades sharply.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Ambient immersion layer word density; difficulty calibration.
**Application:** The ambient word density in Layer 1 is calibrated so that the proportion of unknown words does not exceed the comprehension-breakdown threshold, keeping the immersion experience accessible rather than frustrating.

---

### [Webb, 2007] — Repetition and Vocabulary Knowledge
**Full citation:** Webb, S. (2007). The effects of repetition on vocabulary knowledge. *Applied Linguistics, 28*(1), 46–65.
**Key finding:** Multiple encounters with a word in context are required for full vocabulary knowledge — approximately 10–12 encounters across different aspects of word knowledge (form, meaning, use, collocations).
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** FSRS review scheduling; multi-layer encounter design.
**Application:** The FSRS scheduler ensures each word is encountered in multiple sessions, and the three-layer architecture provides encounters across different modalities (ambient exposure, recognition, production), implementing Webb's multi-encounter requirement.

## SECTION D — Neuroscience of Language Acquisition

### [Kuhl, 2010] — Brain Mechanisms in Early Language Acquisition
**Full citation:** Kuhl, P.K. (2010). Brain mechanisms in early language acquisition. *Neuron, 67*(5), 713–727.
**Key finding:** Early phonological learning establishes neural commitments that shape subsequent language processing; social interaction amplifies phonological learning beyond what audio exposure alone produces.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** IPA display; audio playback on quiz cards.
**Application:** Audio pronunciation is provided for every vocabulary item alongside IPA transcription, implementing Kuhl's finding that phonological encoding (not just orthographic) is required for genuine language learning.

---

### [Mechelli et al., 2004] — Structural Plasticity in the Bilingual Brain
**Full citation:** Mechelli, A., Crinion, J.T., Noppeney, U., O'Doherty, J., Ashburner, J., Frackowiak, R.S., & Price, C.J. (2004). Neurolinguistics: Structural plasticity in the bilingual brain. *Nature, 431*(7010), 757.
**Key finding:** Bilingual speakers show increased grey matter density in the left inferior parietal cortex compared to monolinguals, with the effect being larger in early and more proficient bilinguals — demonstrating that language learning induces measurable structural brain change.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Framing of the language learning mode as a meaningful long-term practice.
**Application:** Onboarding copy can honestly reference neuroplasticity evidence (Mechelli et al.) as support for the claim that language learning changes the brain — this finding has replicated more robustly than the executive-function bilingual advantage.

---

### [Abutalebi & Green, 2007] — Neurocognition of Bilingual Language Control
**Full citation:** Abutalebi, J., & Green, D. (2007). Bilingual language production: The neurocognition of language representation and control. *Journal of Neurolinguistics, 20*(3), 242–275.
**Key finding:** Managing two languages engages a language-control network overlapping with general executive control — specifically anterior cingulate cortex and basal ganglia — providing a neural mechanism for the proposed bilingual cognitive advantage.
**Relevance tier:** MODERATE
**Game mechanic:** Matrix A/B toggle as attentional switching mechanic.
**Application:** The toggle mechanic exercises the same inhibitory-control circuits identified by Abutalebi and Green, providing a principled neural basis for the mechanic even absent guaranteed cognitive transfer outcomes.

---

### [Patel, 2008] — Music, Language, and the Brain
**Full citation:** Patel, A.D. (2008). *Music, Language, and the Brain*. Oxford University Press.
**Key finding:** Musical rhythm training accelerates phonological acquisition because music and language share neural resources for temporal processing, beat perception, and prosodic encoding.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Rhythm mode integration with language learning; audio-synchronized ambient layer.
**Application:** The Rhythm mode is integrated with the Language Learning mode so that target-language syllables are synchronized to rhythmic beats. Patel's OPERA hypothesis directly justifies this integration as a mechanism for phonological circuit building, not merely an aesthetic choice.

## SECTION E — Output, Production, and Speaking Early

### [Swain, 1985] — Comprehensible Output Hypothesis
**Full citation:** Swain, M. (1985). Communicative competence: Some roles of comprehensible input and comprehensible output in its development. In S. Gass & C. Madden (Eds.), *Input in Second Language Acquisition* (pp. 235–253). Newbury House.
**Key finding:** Comprehensible input alone is insufficient for full acquisition — learners must also produce comprehensible output, because production forces attention to gaps in grammatical and lexical knowledge that input processing alone does not require.
**Relevance tier:** CRITICAL
**Game mechanic:** Layer 3 production quizzes; bidirectional translation challenge.
**Application:** Layer 3 production quizzes are a required component of the three-layer architecture, not an optional add-on. Swain's output hypothesis directly justifies their inclusion and the 2:1 recognition-to-production ratio that ensures production practice occurs regularly.

---

### [Swain, 1995] — Three Functions of Output in SLA
**Full citation:** Swain, M. (1995). Three functions of output in second language learning. In G. Cook & B. Seidlhofer (Eds.), *Principle and Practice in Applied Linguistics* (pp. 125–144). Oxford University Press.
**Key finding:** Output serves three distinct functions in acquisition: noticing gaps in knowledge, hypothesis-testing about target-language forms, and deepening metalinguistic reflection — none of which are served by input alone.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Layer 3 production quizzes; post-answer reflection display.
**Application:** After a production quiz, the game displays the correct form alongside the player's attempt, enabling gap-noticing and metalinguistic reflection consistent with Swain's three output functions.

---

### [Schmidt, 1990] — The Noticing Hypothesis
**Full citation:** Schmidt, R. (1990). The role of consciousness in second language learning. *Applied Linguistics, 11*(2), 129–158.
**Key finding:** Conscious attention to form — noticing — is a necessary condition for acquisition; subconscious exposure alone is insufficient, and learners must notice the gap between their interlanguage and the target form for intake to occur.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Salient vocabulary display in immersion layer; highlighted target word in example sentences.
**Application:** Words in the ambient layer are rendered visibly and legibly, never as background texture. The target word in every example sentence is highlighted, implementing Schmidt's requirement that form be consciously noticed rather than merely subliminally present.

---

### [Mackey, 1999] — Input, Interaction, and Second Language Development
**Full citation:** Mackey, A. (1999). Input, interaction, and second language development: An empirical study of question formation in ESL. *Studies in Second Language Acquisition, 21*(4), 557–587.
**Key finding:** Interactional feedback — responses that implicitly or explicitly indicate a communication breakdown — accelerates acquisition of morphosyntax significantly beyond exposure to the same input without feedback.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Immediate post-answer feedback in quiz layers.
**Application:** Both Layer 2 and Layer 3 provide immediate feedback on incorrect responses, showing the correct form in context. This implements the interactional feedback mechanism Mackey identifies as the driver of acquisition over passive exposure.

## SECTION F — Motivation and Long-Term Retention

### [Dörnyei, 2009] — The L2 Motivational Self System
**Full citation:** Dörnyei, Z. (2009). The L2 motivational self system. In Z. Dörnyei & E. Ushioda (Eds.), *Motivation, Language Identity and the L2 Self* (pp. 9–42). Multilingual Matters.
**Key finding:** Long-term language learning motivation is best predicted by the vividness of the learner's Ideal L2 Self — a mental image of the self using the language fluently — rather than by instrumental or integrative orientation alone.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Daily session streak; language selector with aspirational framing.
**Application:** The language selector and session design emphasize the player's imagined fluent future self through dreamscape immersion aesthetics, building identity-based motivation consistent with Dörnyei's L2 Motivational Self System.

---

### [Csikszentmihalyi, 1990] — Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience
**Full citation:** Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1990). *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience*. Harper & Row.
**Key finding:** Optimal experience occurs when task difficulty precisely matches skill level — producing a flow state characterized by full absorption, loss of self-consciousness, and intrinsic motivation to continue.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** FSRS difficulty calibration; daily session design.
**Application:** FSRS continuously calibrates the Difficulty (D) parameter per card, keeping each review at the learner's challenge threshold — the precise condition for flow as described by Csikszentmihalyi. Session length is capped to maintain flow rather than produce fatigue.

---

### [Ryan & Deci, 2000] — Self-Determination Theory
**Full citation:** Ryan, R.M., & Deci, E.L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. *American Psychologist, 55*(1), 68–78.
**Key finding:** Intrinsic motivation is supported by three basic psychological needs — autonomy, competence, and relatedness — and is undermined by controlling external rewards that reduce perceived self-determination.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Gamification design (streaks, progress tracking); learning style selector.
**Application:** Points and streaks are framed as progress tracking (competence support), not as rewards controlling behavior. The learning style selector provides autonomy. Gamification elements are designed to feel like tools, not manipulation — consistent with Section I's contested gamification findings.

---

### [Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999] — Extrinsic Rewards and Intrinsic Motivation
**Full citation:** Deci, E.L., Koestner, R., & Ryan, R.M. (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. *Psychological Bulletin, 125*(6), 627–668.
**Key finding:** A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that tangible, expected, contingent rewards reliably undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks — an effect that is robust across age groups and reward types.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** Gamification design; streaks and progress tracking.
**Application:** This finding is the primary constraint on the gamification design: points, streaks, and scores must be framed as self-monitoring information rather than as contingent rewards. The distinction between informational and controlling reward presentation is the operationalized response to Deci et al.'s meta-analytic finding.

---

### [Gardner & Lambert, 1972] — Attitudes and Motivation in Second-Language Learning
**Full citation:** Gardner, R.C., & Lambert, W.E. (1972). *Attitudes and Motivation in Second-Language Learning*. Newbury House.
**Key finding:** Integrative motivation — a genuine desire to identify with the target-language community — predicts long-term success in language learning more reliably than instrumental motivation alone.
**Relevance tier:** MODERATE
**Game mechanic:** Dreamscape aesthetic and cultural immersion framing.
**Application:** The dreamscape environment presents target-language content within culturally evocative aesthetic contexts, building integrative motivation by making the language feel like a gateway to a world rather than a test to pass.

## SECTION G — Accelerated and Polymath Learning

### [Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, 1993] — The Role of Deliberate Practice
**Full citation:** Ericsson, K.A., Krampe, R.T., & Tesch-Römer, C. (1993). The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance. *Psychological Review, 100*(3), 363–406.
**Key finding:** Expert performance is predicted not by raw practice time but by deliberate practice — practice that targets specific weaknesses with immediate, precise feedback, requiring full concentration.
**Relevance tier:** HIGH
**Game mechanic:** FSRS D/S/R feedback loop; error-focused review scheduling.
**Application:** The post-review display of D, S, and R values constitutes the immediate feedback on memory state required for deliberate practice. FSRS prioritizes high-Difficulty cards for review, targeting specific weaknesses rather than reviewing everything uniformly.

### [Ferriss, 2012] — The 4-Hour Chef (DiSSS Method)

## SECTION I — Contested Findings (Required Honest Section)

This section documents areas where the research is genuinely disputed. Game design decisions must acknowledge these limits rather than overclaiming on contested evidence.

### I.1 — The Bilingual Advantage Controversy
Bialystok's executive function advantage for bilinguals — cited in Section A — has not replicated consistently across independent labs. Paap et al. (2015) and de Bruin et al. (2015) found predominantly null results in large-scale replication attempts. The effect appears to be real in some populations (particularly older adults and clinical dementia samples) and absent in others. **Game design implication:** The Language Learning mode must not advertise itself as "making you smarter" or "boosting executive function." The Matrix A/B toggle is framed as attentional exercise with uncertain transfer, not as a guaranteed cognitive enhancer. The bilingual advantage section of this document includes both the original claim (Bialystok et al., 2007) and the replication failures (Paap et al., 2015) with equal weight.

### I.2 — Krashen's Input Hypothesis
The i+1 input hypothesis is the most widely cited framework in SLA and has been enormously influential in communicative language teaching. It is also largely unfalsifiable as stated: "comprehensible input" and "i+1" cannot be independently operationalized and measured, making the hypothesis resistant to disconfirmation. Output researchers (Swain, 1985, 1995) demonstrated that input alone is insufficient — production and interactional feedback are also necessary components of acquisition. Interaction researchers (Long, Mackey) similarly demonstrated that meaning negotiation accelerates acquisition beyond what input alone predicts. **Game implication:** The mode includes all three components — input, recognition, and production — rather than input-only immersion. The ambient layer is not sufficient alone; Layers 2 and 3 are required for acquisition, not optional.

### I.3 — Spaced Repetition for Vocabulary and Grammar
Spaced repetition systems, including FSRS, are well-validated for form-meaning pair recall — knowing that *le chat* means "the cat." The evidence is substantially weaker for productive use in context, collocational knowledge, grammatical accuracy, and discourse-level competence. FSRS optimizes retrievability of discrete items, not production fluency or communicative competence. **Acknowledged design limit:** The Language Learning mode is designed for vocabulary acquisition. It does not claim to develop grammar, pragmatic competence, or discourse fluency. These require interactional and production practice the mode does not provide.

### I.4 — Gamification Effects on Intrinsic Motivation
Deterding et al. (2011) and subsequent meta-analyses show mixed results for gamification in educational contexts. Most critically, Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) demonstrated in a meta-analysis that external rewards — including points, badges, and streaks — can undermine intrinsic motivation when they are perceived as controlling rather than informational. Extrinsic reward systems risk replacing intrinsic enjoyment with score-chasing, degrading long-term engagement. **Implication:** Points and streaks in the Language Learning mode must be designed to feel like self-monitoring tools (informational) rather than behavioral manipulation (controlling). The visual design of progress displays emphasizes personal history over competitive ranking.

## Design Principles

The five most important evidence-based decisions for the Language Learning mode, for the agent implementing LANG1.

**1. The three-layer architecture is non-negotiable.** The mode requires three components — ambient immersion (Layer 1), recognition quiz (Layer 2), and production quiz (Layer 3) — because the research base for any single-component approach is insufficient. Krashen's input hypothesis alone is contested and unfalsifiable; Swain demonstrated output is necessary; Schmidt demonstrated noticing requires conscious attention. Skipping any layer removes a mechanism that the evidence supports as necessary for acquisition. The ratio is 2:1 recognition-to-production, with production gated on prior recognition.

**2. FSRS-5 is the only acceptable scheduler.** SM-2 achieves approximately 47% retention. FSRS achieves approximately 90% with fewer reviews. There is no principled reason to accept a 43-percentage-point deficit in retention when the better algorithm is open-source and implemented. FSRS D, S, and R values must be visible to players after each review — this implements the deliberate practice requirement (Ericsson et al., 1993) that learners receive precise, immediate feedback on specific performance dimensions.

**3. Every vocabulary item appears in a complete sentence, never as an isolated form-meaning pair.** This is supported by Lomb (2008), Nation (2001), Laufer and Nation (1999), VanPatten (1996), and Webb (2007) from multiple independent research traditions. Isolated word lists are categorically excluded from the design. The example sentence must be present in the ambient display, the recognition quiz card, and the production quiz prompt.

**4. The sleep gate is a hard constraint, not a preference.** Stickgold (2005) and Walker (2017) establish that declarative memory consolidation requires sleep-stage processing of a manageable new-item load. The daily new-word limit enforces the biology: players cannot unlock additional new words within a single session. FSRS can optimize review of already-seen items; it cannot compensate for skipped sleep consolidation. The limit is the correct implementation of the underlying neuroscience.

**5. Gamification must be informational, not controlling.** Ryan and Deci's (2000) self-determination theory and the contested gamification findings in Section I.4 converge on the same implication: external rewards undermine intrinsic motivation when perceived as controlling. Streaks, session counts, and vocabulary totals are displayed as self-monitoring tools showing the player their own progress history. They are not competitive rankings, achievement badges, or social comparisons. The goal is to support autonomous motivation, not to manufacture artificial engagement through reward schedules.
