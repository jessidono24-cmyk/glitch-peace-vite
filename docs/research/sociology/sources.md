# Sociology Research Sources

## Sources (minimum 10)

### [Durkheim, 1897] — Suicide: A Study in Sociology
**Full citation:** Durkheim, É. (1897). *Le Suicide: Étude de Sociologie* [Suicide: A Study in Sociology]. Félix Alcan. (Trans. Spaulding, J.A., & Simpson, G. (1951). Free Press.)
**Key finding:** Social integration and moral regulation determine suicide rates across populations; anomie (normlessness) and egoism (social disconnection) are primary risk conditions.
**Game mechanic:** Recovery mode — social connection health indicator.
**Application:** The player's recovery health responds to social engagement scores; prolonged solo play without community interaction triggers an "anomie warning" and bonus social challenge events, based on Durkheim's isolation–risk relationship.

### [Bourdieu, 1986] — The Forms of Capital
**Full citation:** Bourdieu, P. (1986). The forms of capital. In J. Richardson (Ed.), *Handbook of Theory and Research for the Sociology of Education* (pp. 241–258). Greenwood.
**Key finding:** Social capital (network relationships), cultural capital (education/cultural knowledge), and economic capital interact to reproduce social position; cultural capital can be converted to social advantage.
**Game mechanic:** RPG mode — social capital accumulation.
**Application:** Relational choices in RPG mode build social capital (alliances) or cultural capital (knowledge items); at decision points, the type of capital held determines which paths are accessible — modeling Bourdieu's capital-conversion dynamics.

### [Putnam, 2000] — Bowling Alone
**Full citation:** Putnam, R.D. (2000). *Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community*. Simon & Schuster.
**Key finding:** Decline in civic participation and face-to-face community engagement ("social capital") correlates with increased depression, crime, and health decline.
**Game mechanic:** Community health tile in Recovery mode.
**Application:** The player's dreamscape has a Community Health tile cluster; neglecting community interactions degrades these tiles over time, mirroring Putnam's evidence that social capital is a health-protective resource.

### [Goffman, 1959] — The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life
**Full citation:** Goffman, E. (1959). *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*. Anchor Books.
**Key finding:** Social interaction is performative (dramaturgical); people manage "front stage" impressions while protecting "back stage" authentic selves — the distinction is universal.
**Game mechanic:** RPG mode — front stage / back stage identity split.
**Application:** The RPG mode gives players a public identity (front stage archetype visible to other characters) and a private state (back stage emotional health invisible to NPCs), modeling Goffman's dramaturgical distinction.

### [Becker, 1963] — Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance
**Full citation:** Becker, H.S. (1963). *Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance*. Free Press.
**Key finding:** Deviance is not an intrinsic property of acts but a label applied by social groups with power to define norms; labeling creates secondary deviance by altering self-concept.
**Game mechanic:** Recovery mode — no moralized labeling.
**Application:** The recovery mode explicitly avoids labeling tiles or player states as "bad" or "deviant"; DESPAIR tiles are described in neutral functional terms, following Becker's warning against labeling effects on self-concept.

### [Festinger, 1957] — A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance
**Full citation:** Festinger, L. (1957). *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*. Stanford University Press.
**Key finding:** When beliefs and behaviors conflict, people experience aversive cognitive dissonance; they resolve it by changing beliefs, behaviors, or perception — not necessarily in the healthiest direction.
**Game mechanic:** Recovery mode — cognitive dissonance mechanic.
**Application:** The consequence preview system is designed to create mild, conscious dissonance between player values (PEACE archetype) and impulsive RAGE tile collection, making the dissonance explicit to encourage behavior change rather than rationalization.

### [Merton, 1938] — Social Structure and Anomie
**Full citation:** Merton, R.K. (1938). Social structure and anomie. *American Sociological Review*, 3(5), 672–682.
**Key finding:** Strain theory: when culturally valued goals (success) are inaccessible through legitimate means, individuals adopt deviant adaptations (innovation, retreatism, rebellion, ritualism).
**Game mechanic:** Recovery mode — alternative pathway mechanics.
**Application:** The recovery system ensures alternative PEACE-tile pathways are always available; blocking all legitimate paths and leaving only DESPAIR routes reproduces Merton's strain, so the game design explicitly prevents this scenario.

### [Coleman, 1988] — Social Capital in the Creation of Human Capital
**Full citation:** Coleman, J.S. (1988). Social capital in the creation of human capital. *American Journal of Sociology*, 94 (Suppl.), S95–S120.
**Key finding:** Dense, trust-filled social networks (high social capital) support children's educational attainment; obligations, expectations, and information channels are the mechanisms.
**Game mechanic:** Community mode — trust-network scoring.
**Application:** When players invest in community interactions, a trust network forms that provides information advantages (early warning of DESPAIR clusters, bonus resource flows), directly modeling Coleman's social capital information-channel mechanism.

### [Wuthnow, 1994] — Sharing the Journey: Support Groups and America's New Quest for Community
**Full citation:** Wuthnow, R. (1994). *Sharing the Journey: Support Groups and America's New Quest for Community*. Free Press.
**Key finding:** Small support groups provide emotional resources and identity scaffolding that larger institutions cannot; participation restructures emotional experience through collective meaning-making.
**Game mechanic:** Recovery mode — support group event mechanics.
**Application:** The "support circle" event in Recovery mode presents a rotating cast of NPC characters sharing their tile-collection struggles; collective meaning-making within the circle yields health bonuses that solo play cannot achieve.

### [Bronfenbrenner, 1979] — The Ecology of Human Development
**Full citation:** Bronfenbrenner, U. (1979). *The Ecology of Human Development: Experiments by Nature and Design*. Harvard University Press.
**Key finding:** Human development is shaped by nested environmental systems (microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, macrosystem); well-being depends on bidirectional interactions across all system levels.
**Game mechanic:** Recovery mode — multi-system health tracking.
**Application:** The recovery dashboard tracks health across four nested levels: personal (tile skills), relational (NPC interactions), community (group events), and structural (dreamscape ecology), modeling Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems.
